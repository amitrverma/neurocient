# IKEA Framework

## Summary
The Practice product is built on **IKEA + Reinforcement + Behavioral Design**.

IKEA is the behavior-change spine:

- **Identity**: who the user believes they are, and what feels consistent or inconsistent with that self-image.
- **Knowledge**: what the user comes to understand about why the behavior exists; the hidden motive, fear, reward, protection, or emotional logic.
- **Environment**: the context that quietly makes the old behavior easier and the new behavior harder.
- **Action**: the smallest concrete move that fits the understood pattern.
- **Reinforcement**: repeated retrieval and practice over time, because forgetting is real.
- **Behavioral Design**: deliberate use of cognitive biases, defaults, salience, memory, friction, and reward to make useful behavior easier to repeat.

The core belief:

> People do not change because they receive the right advice. They change when insight becomes emotionally light enough, retrievable enough, repeated enough, and environmentally supported enough to turn into behavior.

Practice should work with how attention, memory, emotion, and shortcuts actually operate. Cognitive biases are design material, but they must be used ethically: to reduce friction, increase clarity, make progress visible, and help users keep promises they already want to keep. Do not use biases as dark patterns, pressure tactics, or hidden manipulation.

## Story Spine
Practice should be experienced as a story of a lived behavior loop, not as a lesson or a feature sequence.

For procrastination, the story begins with actual moments:

- waiting until the deadline is close
- switching to an easier activity
- delaying a decision
- walking past the same task several times
- keeping a document unopened because opening it makes the task feel real

The product should help the user recognize themselves in concrete scenes before introducing frameworks or labels. Insights should be compressed into moments, not delivered as lessons.

## Identity
Identity is where cognitive dissonance matters.

Users often carry self-stories such as:

- "I am lazy."
- "I only work under pressure."
- "I am bad at finishing."
- "I am just not disciplined."

The product should help users see these as stories or adaptations, not fixed traits.

Identity work should:

- reduce shame
- expose the current self-story
- offer a more accurate reframe
- make the next action feel consistent with a better identity

Avoid:

- moralizing
- motivational pressure
- "just become the kind of person who..." language

## Knowledge
Knowledge asks why the behavior makes sense.

For procrastination, delay may be protecting the user from:

- failure
- success and raised expectations
- ambiguity
- overwhelm
- emotional discomfort
- low immediate reward
- decision fatigue

Knowledge should make the user feel:

- "That explains it."
- "I am not broken."
- "There is a reason this keeps happening."

Avoid jumping directly from symptom to advice.

## Environment
Environment is not decoration; it is part of behavior.

People underestimate how much their surroundings, defaults, cues, friction, timing, devices, social context, and workspace affect action.

Every practice should eventually ask:

- What makes the old loop easy?
- What makes the useful action hard?
- What can be changed outside the user, not just inside them?

Examples:

- keep the document open before a planned work block
- remove the phone from reach
- make the first action visible
- pre-decide the time and place
- reduce setup friction
- create a cue that catches the user in the moment

## Action
Action comes after Identity, Knowledge, and Environment.

Good actions are:

- tiny
- concrete
- emotionally light
- observable
- matched to the discovered cause
- possible today

Poor actions are:

- generic
- performative
- too ambitious
- detached from the root cause
- dependent on willpower alone

For procrastination:

- "Be more disciplined" is bad.
- "Open the task and write one ugly first sentence" is better.
- "Write the next physical action another person could watch you do" is better.

## Reinforcement
Insight decays unless it is retrieved.

Reinforcement exists because the forgetting curve is real. The product must help users repeatedly retrieve the useful insight in the moments where the old pattern returns.

Reinforcement should include:

- spaced repetition
- short check-ins
- reminders of the user's discovered pattern
- visible moments of victory
- evidence of behavioral persistence
- reframing progress visually

Do not treat reinforcement as generic notifications. A good reminder returns the person to the insight:

> "Yesterday you noticed procrastination appears when the next action is unclear. Is that happening again?"

## Behavioral Design And Cognitive Biases
Practice should heavily use cognitive biases to drive behavior, while preserving user trust and emotional safety.

Useful biases and design levers include:

- **Present bias**: make the useful first move immediately small, visible, and rewarding.
- **Loss aversion**: frame the cost as less life lost to autopilot, without shame or panic.
- **Identity and consistency bias**: help the user act in ways that match a healthier self-story.
- **Availability bias**: make recent wins and recognized patterns easy to recall.
- **Commitment effect**: ask for tiny, concrete commitments the user can plausibly keep.
- **Default effect**: make the next useful step the easiest path through the interface.
- **Zeigarnik effect**: use visible unfinished first steps to gently pull the user back.
- **Peak-end rule**: end sessions with a moment of recognition, relief, or victory.
- **Fresh start effect**: create natural re-entry points after forgetting, so return feels normal.
- **Salience bias**: make the real behavior pattern vivid enough to notice in daily life.

The design goal is not to trick the user into engagement. The goal is to make emotionally useful behavior easier than the old loop.

## Flow Order
Practice experiences should generally move through:

1. Recognition: "This is the loop."
2. Identity: "This is the story it creates about me."
3. Knowledge: "This is why the loop makes sense."
4. Environment: "This is what supports the loop."
5. Action: "This is the smallest useful move."
6. Reinforcement: "This is how the insight comes back."
7. Moment of victory: "This is what changed, however small."

This order is a default path, not a rigid procedure. Mature Practice experiences should adapt based on what the user recognizes, what they have already tried, whether it helped, what their environment supports, and how they respond to reinforcement.

## Product Implication
The Practice product is not a challenge app in the conventional sense.

It is a behavior-change companion that turns self-understanding into emotionally sustainable practice.
